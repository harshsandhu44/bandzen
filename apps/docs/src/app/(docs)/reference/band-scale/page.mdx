# The band scale

IELTS reports on a 0–9 scale in half-band steps. Bandzen estimates on the same
scale so its figures are comparable to the ones you are aiming for.

<Ruler className="my-8" />

## Reading and Listening

Both are marked by the same pure function, on the same conversion table. Your
raw score is scaled to 40 first, so a 13-question practice set reports on the
same scale as a full test. Then it converts:

This is the public Academic Reading conversion. It is currently applied to
General Training candidates too, and to Listening, and all of those convert
more generously in the real exam — so a General Training or Listening band
from Bandzen is conservative.

A second set of four criteria, specific to Speaking:

For both Writing and Speaking, the overall band is the **mean of the four,
rounded to the nearest half band**. It is not a separate judgement made on top
of them, so a report where three criteria are 6.5 and one is 5.0 lands at 6.0 —
and the 5.0 is the thing to work on.

## Your overall estimate

The mean of every module you have a band for.

Not always the same four IELTS averages. Where you have attempted all four, a
Bandzen overall estimate is comparable in shape to an IELTS overall band;
where you have only attempted some, it is a mean of fewer things, and worth
reading as what it is — Reading and Writing only, say, rather than a full
picture.

## What a band from Bandzen is not

<Callout title="An estimate, not a score">
  It is produced by this product from work you did here. It is not an IELTS
  score, it does not come from IELTS, and it does not predict what you will get
  on the day. Writing bands in particular come from a language model reading
  against the published descriptors, and two markings of the same essay can
  differ.
</Callout>

Read the movement, not the point. One essay half a band above the last is inside
the noise. Five attempts trending upward is a signal.
